# Launch Metro Selection

Status: draft, September 2026. Recommendation at the end.

## The reframe

The question is not "where are the most gardeners" or even "where is growth
fastest." It is: **where is someone already being paid to redo their landscape,
and required to submit a plan to collect?**

That turns out to narrow the field sharply, and it points somewhere different
than population growth alone would.

## Why growth rate is the wrong primary metric

Population growth is the obvious lens: new homeowners, new yards, new spending.
The fastest-growing metros in the year to July 2025 were roughly:

| Metro | Growth |
|---|---|
| Austin–Round Rock, TX | ~3.2% |
| Raleigh–Cary, NC | ~2.8% |
| Boise City, ID | ~2.6% |
| St. George, UT | ~2.5% |
| Phoenix–Mesa–Chandler, AZ | ~2.4% |
| Nashville, TN | ~2.3% |

(Approximate, from Census estimates as reported in spring 2026.)

A new homeowner is a warm prospect for *any* home improvement product. They are
not specifically a prospect for ours. What makes someone a prospect for
NativePlants is a reason to change their landscape and a constraint on what they
can put in. Growth supplies the first and nothing of the second.

## The metric that actually sorts the field

**Arid-West water policy has created a regulated market for exactly our
product.** Multiple states now pay homeowners per square foot to remove lawn,
and most require an approved plan before any grass comes out.

| State | Program shape |
|---|---|
| Utah | Nation's first **statewide** turf rebate. Up to $3/sq ft. 60+ communities. Localscapes Rewards includes plan review. |
| Nevada | SNWA up to $5/sq ft. State law requires removal of decorative grass in commercial, multifamily, and HOA areas by 2027. |
| Colorado | State grant program plus ~15 active local programs. Denver pays $3.25/sq ft for ColoradoScape plantings. |
| California | Metropolitan Water District $2/sq ft, up to 5,000 sq ft per year. |
| Arizona | City-level programs — Phoenix, Tempe, Scottsdale, Glendale, Flagstaff. Phoenix entered Stage 2 Drought Response in early 2026, limiting outdoor irrigation to twice weekly. Arizona faces mandatory 27% Colorado River cuts from 2027. |

A homeowner holding a rebate approval is a fundamentally better prospect than a
generic new homeowner. They have a deadline, a budget already allocated, a
compliance requirement, and a problem they cannot solve from a big-box plant
rack.

## Scoring framework

Proposed weights. These are judgment calls, offered to be argued with.

| Metric | Weight | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Forcing function | 30% | Rebate dollars, plan requirement, program scale. The strongest predictor of someone needing this today. |
| Gardening difficulty | 20% | Does generic advice fail here? Extreme soil pH, aridity, erratic frost. Where gardening is easy, our advantage shrinks. |
| Founder proximity | 15% | For a V1 whose bottlenecks are curation and nursery relationships, being able to drive there beats a point of growth. |
| Curation cost | 10% | How many species to cover the market credibly. Fewer ecoregions is cheaper. |
| Nursery density and independence | 10% | Independent garden centers are the Phase 4 supply side. Chains are not partners. |
| Reviewer availability | 5% | A land-grant extension program with published soil guidance means a credible horticultural reviewer exists locally. |
| Growth and new homeowners | 5% | Real, but secondary. Demoted deliberately. |
| Competitive saturation | 5% | How strong are the free alternatives here already. |

## Candidates

### Salt Lake City / Wasatch Front

**Forcing function: strongest in the country.** Utah runs the first statewide
turf rebate in the US. In 2024 alone, roughly 3 million square feet of lawn were
converted and over $7 million paid out. Localscapes Rewards requires a submitted
landscape plan and specifies design standards — 50% plant coverage at maturity,
3–4 inches of mulch, drip irrigation on dedicated zones. The program has a
documented spring bottleneck, with temporary closures when demand outruns
processing.

Read that again: **the state requires a compliant landscape plan, and the plan
process is a known bottleneck.** That is a product-shaped hole.

**Gardening difficulty: high and well documented.** Dense alkaline clay
averaging around pH 8.0, high-elevation sun, dry air, erratic spring frosts.
Generic national plant advice fails here routinely — the acid-loving plants sold
at big-box stores simply do not work.

**Proximity: you live there.**

**Reviewer availability: strong.** USU Extension publishes extensively on Utah
clay soils and has a horticulture program to recruit from.

**The honest problem: growth is slow.** SLC metro is around 1.26 million and
grew roughly 1% in the most recent estimate. The new-homeowner thesis does not
hold here. The market is also small in absolute terms.

**The offset:** St. George / Washington County grew about 2.5% and ranked 8th
fastest-growing metro in the country, the only Western metro in the top 10. Same
state, same rebate program, same regulatory context. The plant palette differs —
St. George is hotter and drier, a Mojave transition rather than Wasatch Front —
so it is a second curation pass, not a free one. But everything non-botanical
carries over.

### Austin

**The strongest native plant infrastructure in the country.** The Native Plant
Society of Texas has 4,000+ members across 34 chapters, and the Lady Bird
Johnson Wildflower Center is physically in Austin. Growth is the fastest of any
large metro at roughly 3.2%.

**Two problems.** First, no forcing function — Texas has no comparable statewide
rebate, so nobody is being paid or required to redo their yard. Second, that
same infrastructure means the free alternatives are strongest here. We would be
launching where the incumbents are best.

**Curation cost is also higher:** Austin straddles two ecoregions, Blackland
Prairie and Edwards Plateau, divided roughly by I-35, with genuinely different
soils on each side. That is close to two plant palettes for one metro.

Austin is the best *second* market and a strong partnership target. It is a hard
first market.

### Phoenix

**The forcing function is arriving, not arrived.** Stage 2 Drought Response is
live, Arizona faces 27% Colorado River cuts from 2027, and rebates exist at city
level. The pressure is real and increasing. But the programs are fragmented
across municipalities rather than statewide, which means more integration work
for less coverage.

Worth watching. The 2027 cuts could make Phoenix the best market in the country
within two years.

### Denver

A credible alternative. $3.25/sq ft in Denver, a state grant program, roughly 15
active local programs, and an active Colorado Native Plant Society. Similar
alkaline soil challenges. The main argument against it is proximity — everything
Denver offers, the Wasatch Front offers with a stronger statewide program and
you already living there.

### Boise, Raleigh, California

- **Boise** — good growth at 2.6%, but a small market and thinner rebate
  infrastructure.
- **Raleigh** — 2.8% growth, but humid east, acidic soils, no water forcing
  function. A genuinely different product.
- **California** — largest market, real rebates, but slow growth, the most
  competitive landscape, and enormous ecoregion complexity. Not a first market.

## Recommendation

**Launch on the Wasatch Front. Treat St. George as market two.**

The reasoning, in order of weight:

1. **Utah's statewide rebate is the strongest forcing function in the US, and it
   requires a plan.** We are not asking homeowners to want something new. We are
   showing up where they already have money allocated, a compliance requirement,
   and a documented bottleneck in getting approved.
2. **The soil problem is real and generic advice fails on it.** pH 8.0 alkaline
   clay is exactly the case where a national plant database gives wrong answers
   and a soil-survey-driven tool gives right ones. Our differentiator is at its
   most visible here.
3. **You can drive to every nursery and extension office in the market.** For a
   V1 gated on curation and relationships, that is worth more than two points of
   population growth.
4. **The market is small enough to saturate.** 1.26 million people is a
   liability for a venture-scale story and an asset for a bootstrapped one. You
   can plausibly reach most of the relevant gardeners.
5. **St. George gives a second market inside the same regulatory and
   relationship footprint** — and it is genuinely one of the fastest-growing
   metros in the country.

**Accept this tradeoff knowingly:** you are giving up growth rate. SLC at ~1% is
not a boom market, and the new-homeowner thesis does not apply. You are trading
it for regulatory pull, a harder problem that showcases the product, and
operational proximity. For a two-person bootstrapped start, that is the right
trade. For a venture-backed land grab it would not be.

## What this implies for the V1 spec

Three changes worth making:

1. **Ingest Salt Lake, Utah, Davis, and Weber counties** for SSURGO, not a
   single county. The Wasatch Front is continuous across all four.
2. **Curate against the Localscapes standard, not just survivability.** If our
   plant records carry water-use class and mature coverage area, we can tell a
   homeowner whether their plan hits the 50% coverage requirement. That is a
   feature no competitor has and the state effectively wrote the spec for us.
3. **A rebate-eligibility view becomes the natural V1.5.** Not in V1 scope, but
   design the data model so it is not a rewrite: water-use class and mature
   dimensions on every plant record from the first curation pass.

## Open question for a follow-up

Whether Utah's program publishes a qualifying plant list we can align to, and
whether Localscapes would consider a partnership. If the state already maintains
a waterwise plant list, that is a large chunk of curation we do not have to
originate — and a credibility marker we could not buy.
